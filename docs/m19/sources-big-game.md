## 1. IGFA line classes and fly tippet classes

**The game now.**
- `src/game/Gear.js:11-13`: "IGFA's line classes are 2 to 130 lb, a line breaking at or under its class"; `LB = 0.45359237`.
- `Gear.js:31-37`: the lines are 8 lb mono (3.63 kg), 15 lb mono (6.80 kg), 30 lb braid (13.61 kg), 60 lb braid (27.22 kg) and 80 lb mono (36.29 kg), each breaking at its label (`lineKg: lb × LB`).
- `src/game/FishTable.js:155`: "No fly rod (the user's, M17)". `docs/M19.md:22` and `:31` plan fly rods with tippets, and records for each IGFA line class.

**Sources.**
- **IGFA, International Angling Rules** ([igfa.org](https://igfa.org/international-angling-rules/), opened). The class tables there are images, so I couldn't read them as text. The text names 4 kg (8 lb) and 6 kg (12 lb) as examples, the heaviest line class as 60 kg (130 lb), and the heaviest tippet class as 10 kg (20 lb). If a line or tippet tests over the class maximum the claim is denied. One that tests under a lower class isn't moved down. On the rule itself: "If the line or tippet over tests its class, the application will be considered in the next highest class."
- **IGFA, World Record Requirements** ([igfa.org/?p=2301](https://igfa.org/?p=2301), opened).
  - Samples are tested in metric. A line "may test up to 8.81 pounds (4 kilograms)" and still qualify for the 4 kg (8 lb) class.
  - For a line-class or tippet-class record the fish must weigh at least half the class: a 6 kg class fish at least 2.72 kg (6 lb).
  - A new record must beat the old one by 56.69 g (2 oz) under 11.34 kg (25 lb), and by 0.5% at 11.34 kg (25 lb) or more.
  - Junior (11 to 16) and Smallfry (10 and under) records are kept. The page doesn't mention men's and women's line-class records, but IGFA does keep them: Field & Stream lists a "Men's 37 kg (80 lb) Line Class Record" ([Field & Stream](https://www.fieldandstream.com/photos/gallery/fishing/fly-fishing/where-fish/2012/01/latest-pending-records-igfa), search result only).
- **IGFA, International Angling Rules PDF, 2020** ([PDF](https://igfa.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/IGFA-International-Angling-Rules_May122020.pdf), text extracted here from the PDF).
  - Rule A.1: monofilament, multifilament and lead-core multifilament lines may be used. Braid is a multifilament. Wire is banned and backing is allowed.
  - Rule A.4: the class is the breaking strength of the first 5 m (16.5 ft) of line before the double line, leader or hook, which may not exceed the 60 kg (130 lb) class. So a braid backing under a mono top shot is legal, and the top shot sets the class.
  - Double line and leader: up to 4.57 m (15 ft) each in classes up to 10 kg (20 lb), 6.1 m (20 ft) combined. Over 10 kg, up to 9.14 m (30 ft) each, 12.19 m (40 ft) combined.
  - Fighting chairs "may not have any mechanically propelled devices", and gimbals must swing freely.
  - The angler must hook, fight and land the fish without help. Others may take the leader once it's in the mate's grasp or wound to the rod tip. No rule in it limits the boat backing down or chasing.
  - The newer 2026 PDF ([PDF](https://igfa.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/05/IGFA-Rules-and-Regulations_May-2026.pdf)) downloaded, but its text couldn't be extracted here.
- **The full table, in kg and lb.** Bish on Fish ([bishfish.co.nz](https://bishfish.co.nz/bishonfish/tag/line-classes.html), opened) gives each class and the most it may test: 1 kg = 2 lb (2.2), 2 = 4 (4.4), 3 = 6 (6.61), 4 = 8 (8.81), 6 = 12 (13.22), 8 = 16 (17.63), 10 = 20 (22.04), 15 = 30 (33.06), 24 = 50 (52.91), 37 = 80 (81.57) and 60 = 130 (132.27). The 1 to 10 kg classes are also the tippet classes. Sea Angling Ireland ([sea-angling-ireland.org](https://www.sea-angling-ireland.org/igfa.htm), opened) gives the same list, with tippets 1 to 10 kg (2 to 20 lb). These agree with IGFA's own 8.81 lb for 4 kg.
- **Braid over-tests its label.**
  - Doug Olander pulled 101 lines rated 30 lb on IGFA's Instron, three times each ([Sport Fishing](https://www.sportfishingmag.com/gallery/gear/2014/11/line-test/), opened). The strongest broke at 66.8 lb (30.3 kg), and some broke under 27 lb. "All but a few lines exceeded 30 pounds."
  - Florida Sportsman tested seven braids on IGFA's machine ([Florida Sportsman](https://www.floridasportsman.com/editorial/gear_051148/401542), opened; its results table is an image). Every braid broke above its rating, "in some cases ... more than twice what was listed."
  - Some braids are sold pre-tested to break under an IGFA class: Platypus Pre-test, sold as "37 kg" ([TackleDirect](https://www.tackledirect.com/platypus-pre-test-braid-dark-green-37kg-x-300yd.html), search result only).
  - Braid in the US comes as 50, 65 and 80 lb (PowerPro's sizes, §3). A "60 lb" braid is between them.

- **Does braid sold as "60 lb" fit the 37 kg (80 lb) class?** Only if it really breaks at 37 kg (81.57 lb) or less, i.e. at no more than 1.36 times its label. From the two tests above, many braids test well over their labels, and the strongest went 2.2 times. So a real "60 lb" braid could test into the 60 kg class. In the game it breaks at its label, so it's in the 37 kg class.
- **The least a record fish may weigh** (half the class): 2 kg in the 4 kg class, 4 kg in 8 kg, 7.5 kg in 15 kg, 18.5 kg in 37 kg, 30 kg in 60 kg; 3, 4 and 5 kg in the 6, 8 and 10 kg tippet classes.

**For the game.**
- Classes as IGFA's, in kg with the lb label: 4 kg (8 lb), 8 kg (16 lb), 15 kg (30 lb), 37 kg (80 lb) and 60 kg (130 lb) for the lines; 6, 8 and 10 kg (12, 16, 20 lb) for the tippets.
- 15 lb mono's fish go in the 8 kg (16 lb) class, and 60 lb braid's in the 37 kg (80 lb) class with 80 lb mono's. The alternative is relabelling one of them to suit a class: a "12 lb" line instead of 15, or a "50 lb" braid instead of 60. That changes the fights, which M17 balanced, so it's the user's call.
- A record fish weighs at least half its class. A new record beats the old by 56.69 g (2 oz) under 11.34 kg (25 lb), or by 0.5% above it.
- A tippet record goes by the tippet's class, not the fly line's (IGFA puts no limit on the fly line or backing).

## 4. Backing down on a big fish

**The game now.**
- `src/together/Crew.js:21`: `BACKING = 0.4`, "m/s of line she takes back, backing down at a knot or so". It only comes in when a second player is at the helm (`Crew.js:161-164`).
- `src/game/CatchMinigame.js:49`: the fight takes `away`, the m/s the boat's going from the fish.
- `docs/m17/lines.md:223-239`: from the chair on 80 lb, alone, blue marlin are landed to about 350 kg; with a second player backing down, to about 390 kg. A boat closing at 1.5 m/s with 900 m of line landed the biggest, but that 1.5 m/s was picked for the test.
- `docs/M19.md:12`: a hired mate takes the helm and backs her down.

**Sources.**
- **Sport Fishing, "How to Back Down Safely"** ([sportfishingmag.com/?p=45869](https://www.sportfishingmag.com/?p=45869), opened).
  - Backing down is for sport-fishers of about 40 ft (12 m) and up. Open boats should chase forward instead.
  - In big seas a captain goes "just fast enough" to keep line coming, gives throttle between waves, and eases off as each one reaches the transom. He can go faster once the fish lets him back with the sea.
  - One captain runs two 2,500 gph (9,460 L/h) bilge pumps throughout. Long runs in reverse can overheat engines whose water pickups face forward.
  - When a hot fish threatens to spool the reel, turn and chase it forward with the line at 10 or 2 o'clock, then back down again once there's line on. Turning can cost up to 200 yd (183 m) of line.
  - "No fish is worth risking the lives of your passengers and crew."
- **Boat tests, speed in reverse.**
  - Viking 46 Billfish ([Yachting](https://www.yachtingmagazine.com/story/yachts/viking-46-billfish-reviewed/), opened): "I could back down at 8 or 9 knots with no water coming into the cockpit."
  - Viking 70 ([Marlin](https://www.marlinmag.com/viking-70-convertible), opened): just over 7 knots "in total control", and Capt. Higgins says he has hit 11 knots in reverse fighting fish. Water came over the covering boards only when backing up-sea.
  - Viking 74 ([Marlin](https://www.marlinmag.com/boats/boat-reviews/viking-74/), opened): 7 knots in reverse before any water came over the transom.
  - A Viking 64: 6.5 knots (Marlin, search result only).
  - Hatteras 65 ([Marlin](https://www.marlinmag.com/?p=38544), opened): no backing speed, but "seas occasionally coming over the caprail" without covering the cockpit sole.
  - Viking 52 ([Power & Motoryacht](https://powerandmotoryacht.com/boats/boat-tests/viking-52-2/), opened): in a tournament video, water toppled over the transom when the captain backed down "too hard, too fast".
- **Marlin, boat handling** ([marlinmag.com](https://www.marlinmag.com/boat-handling-tips/), opened). Throttle in short bursts. Chase a green fish forward, and back and spin only near the end: "It's easier to control a hot fish by chasing it in forward gear."
- **Fight times.**
  - Kona, 1995 ([Hawaii Fishing News](https://www.hawaiifishingnews.com/grander_d_ID-453-order-Weight.html), opened): a 1,144 lb (519 kg) blue marlin on 130 lb, about 1 h 40 min, from a 39 ft (11.9 m) Rybovich.
  - Kona, 1979 ([Hawaii Fishing News](https://www.hawaiifishingnews.com/grander_d_ID-393-order-Weight.html), opened): a 1,450 lb (658 kg) blue on 80 lb, 5 h 20 min, from a 41 ft (12.5 m) Hatteras.
  - Mauritius, November 2025 ([Marlin](https://www.marlinmag.com/?p=50967), opened): a 1,072 lb (486 kg) blue on a 130-class Tiagra with 130 lb mono over braid, about 2.5 h. They backed down right after the first long run. With the fish holding deep about 15 m (49 ft) behind, they stayed in reverse for nearly 20 minutes, working it up metre by metre.
  - South Africa ([Outdoor Life](https://www.outdoorlife.com/fishing/giant-blue-marlin-caught-african-coast/), opened): a 1,370 lb (621 kg) blue on an 80-class reel with 130 lb line, about 30 minutes, gaffed green from a 37 ft (11.3 m) Bertram.
  - Kona: a 1,022.5 lb (464 kg) blue, 3.5 h (Marlin, search result only).
  - Marlin, on Logan et al. 2022, *ICES Journal of Marine Science*, Tropic Star Lodge, Panama ([Marlin](https://www.marlinmag.com/travel/understanding-marlin-sailfish-release-recovery-rates-and-survival), opened): "roughly 45 minutes for an average blue marlin fight", against about seven for a sailfish. Fights ran 4 to 90 minutes (search result), and the longer the fight, the longer the recovery.
  - I found no source on Madeira's granders.

**Derived.**
- 6.5 to 9 knots is 3.3 to 4.6 m/s astern, and 11 knots is 5.7 m/s. The crew's 0.4 m/s is 0.78 knots, and M17's test's 1.5 m/s is 2.9 knots.
- Real boats back down 8 to 11 times faster than the crew does in the game. They do it only "just fast enough" in a sea, and in short bursts near the end.
- The line regained is the boat's speed toward the fish less the fish's own way. Against a fish holding deep, as off Mauritius, it's all the boat's.

**For the game.**
- **The mate backs down at up to 3.0 m/s (5.8 knots) on a calm sea.** That's under the 3.3 to 4.6 m/s the tests reached dry, and twice M17's 1.5 m/s. How much it falls as the sea gets up is judgement, e.g. 1.5 m/s in a moderate sea and 0.5 m/s in a rough one, after Sport Fishing's "just fast enough".
- **He chases forward when the line runs low,** and turns back to backing once there's line on (Sport Fishing, Marlin). The threshold, e.g. a quarter of the spool left, is judgement. Turning costs up to 183 m (200 yd) of line (Sport Fishing).
- **He backs down in bursts near the end,** and spins her to keep the fish astern (Marlin): a look, not a figure.
- **A second player at the helm** keeps `BACKING` 0.4 m/s, or uses the mate's figure. That's the user's call.
- **Fight length.** Real fights on these fish take 30 minutes to 2.5 hours, and the average blue marlin fight is 45 minutes. The game's fights are much shorter (M17: a 200 kg blue in 9 minutes). How long a 450 kg fish should take is the user's call.

## 9. Rubber landing nets

**The game now.**
- `src/game/Gear.js:75-79`: landing is your hands (12 kg), a gaff (250 kg), or a dehooker and tag kit.
- `src/game/rigs/Rigs.js:57-58`: a billfish let go by hand out of the water dies 29% of the time.
- `docs/M19.md:22`: a rubber landing net, on its own track.

**Sources.**
- **Barthel, Cooke, Suski and Philipp 2003**, "Effects of landing net mesh type on injury and mortality in a freshwater recreational fishery", *Fisheries Research* 63: 275–282 ([PDF](https://www3.carleton.ca/fecpl/pdfs/Effects_of_nets_on_fish.pdf), text extracted here).
  - Bluegill, 50 to a group, were angled at 26 °C (79 °F) and held in a net for 30 s. The controls were held out of water for the same 30 s, not netted.
  - Deaths in 168 h: control 0%, rubber 4%, knotless nylon 6%, fine knotted 14%, coarse knotted 10%. All of them died between 48 and 120 h.
  - Caudal fin abrasion was worst in the coarse knotted mesh, then the fine knotted and the rubber. Knotless was as low as the controls.
  - Scale and mucus loss in rubber and knotless was as low as the controls. Knotted mesh's was higher.
  - The abstract: "all net types resulted in heightened injury and mortality with the knotted mesh types being more injurious than the rubber or knotless mesh." They urge study on larger fish.
- **Lizée, Lennox, Ward, Brownscombe, Chapman, Danylchuk, Nowell and Cooke 2018**, "Influence of landing net mesh type on handling time and tissue damage of angled Brook Trout", *North American Journal of Fisheries Management* 38: 76–83 ([PDF](https://www.fecpl.ca/wp-content/uploads/2017/10/nafm.10033.pdf), text extracted here).
  - 146 brook trout at 13.7 °C, landed four ways: 25 mm knotless rubber, 2 mm knotless nylon micromesh, 40 mm knotted polypropylene, 6 mm rubber-coated nylon. Bare wet hands were a fifth.
  - Mucus loss: large rubber 7% of fish, nylon 32%, the rest 23 to 30%.
  - Scale loss: nylon 21%, hands 17%, rubber-coated 15%.
  - Only knotted polypropylene frayed fins significantly (odds 5.2 times bare hands). Fins poke through large rubber mesh, so it frayed some.
  - Handling: nylon took longest, 52.6 s, because hooks tangle in it; bare hands were shortest, 23.4 s, but dropped more fish.
  - They conclude large rubber mesh is the best of these.
- **Others** (search results only). Colotelo and Cooke 2011 found knotted nylon caused far more skin damage to northern pike than rubber. A University of Stirling study found rubber-coated mesh almost halved Atlantic salmon's scale loss against knotless (UFAW). De Lestang et al. 2008 compared two nets on barramundi.
- **Prices** (search results only): EGO Wade-Large rubber net, 19 × 21 in (48 × 53 cm) hoop, 30 lb (13.6 kg) maximum load, $84.99. EGO Rubber Mesh Net Large $94.99. Frabill 21 × 24 in (53 × 61 cm) clear rubber conservation net $84.99 (Fleet Farm). The Fly Shop's rubber boat net $85.

**Derived.**
- In Barthel, rubber killed 4 in 100 against knotted mesh's 10 to 14, about a third.
- Both studies are on small freshwater fish. Neither measures billfish or pelagics.

**For the game.**
- **A rubber net lands fish it fits,** up to about 14 kg (30 lb), EGO's rated load. Bigger fish still need the gaff.
- **One let go from it is hurt about 4% of the time** (Barthel's rubber mesh, applied to sea fish by judgement), against 29% for a billfish let go by hand out of the water (`Rigs.js:58`). And quicker to unhook than from a fine mesh (Lizée).
- **$85 to $95** at real prices.

## Numbers for the game

**Line classes (§1)**
- Classes: 4 kg (8 lb), 8 kg (16 lb), 15 kg (30 lb), 37 kg (80 lb) and 60 kg (130 lb) for the lines; 6, 8 and 10 kg (12, 16, 20 lb) for the tippets. The full list is 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 10, 15, 24, 37 and 60 kg.
- Line to class: 8 lb mono → 4 kg; 15 lb mono → 8 kg; 30 lb braid → 15 kg; 60 lb braid → 37 kg; 80 lb mono → 37 kg; 130 lb mono → 60 kg.
- Tippet to class: 12 lb → 6 kg; 16 lb → 8 kg; 20 lb → 10 kg.
- A record fish weighs at least half its class. A new record beats the old by 56.69 g (2 oz) under 11.34 kg (25 lb), and by 0.5% at or over it.

**130-class tackle (§2)**
- 130 lb mono breaks at 58.97 kg. Strike is 22.1 kg and full 31.0 kg.
- The reel: 914 m (1,000 yd) of 130 lb; 45 kg (99 lb) of drag, or 40 kg (88 lb); 99 / 56 cm a turn; 4.9 kg.
- Real prices: the reel $1,470 to $1,520; the bent-butt rod $470 to $800.

**Spool by line (§3)**
- `spoolM = V / d²`.
- V (m·mm²): old spinning 11.2; smooth spinning 28.5; conventional 88; 50-class two-speed 395; 80-class 688; 130-class 1,126.
- d (mm): 8 lb mono 0.25; 15 lb mono 0.36; 30 lb braid 0.31; 60 lb braid 0.41; 80 lb mono 0.89; 130 lb mono 1.11.
- So: 80 lb on the 50-class 499 m; 80 lb on the 80-class 868 m; 130 lb on the 130-class 914 m; 30 lb braid on the conventional 915 m; 60 lb braid on the 50-class 2,350 m.

**Backing down and fight times (§4)**
- Real reverse speeds: 6.5 to 9 knots (3.3 to 4.6 m/s) tested dry, and 11 knots (5.7 m/s) claimed in a fight.
- Turning to chase costs up to 183 m (200 yd) of line.
- Real fights: a 1,000 lb (450 kg) blue on 130 lb in 30 minutes to 2.5 hours; 45 minutes for an average blue marlin.

**Mate (§5)**
- $200 a day out, or $300 for a seasoned one.

**Teasers (§6)**
- A teased fish takes the pitch bait 75% of the time, against 55% off the riggers.
- A set costs about $1,000 at real prices.

**Gyro (§7)**
- A Seakeeper 6-class: $69,600 plus about $23,000 to fit.
- Draws 1.1 to 2.3 kW (use 2 kW) off the generator. Working in 21 minutes.
- 82 to 95% less roll at rest.

**Blue-water speargun (§8)**
- 140 cm (55 in), 4 bands of 14 mm (9/16 in), a 7.5 mm shaft about 1.6 m long, reaching 6 to 7.5 m.
- 4.6 m of shooting line, a breakaway onto 30 m (100 ft) of float line, and an 11 L float. For fish to about 23 kg (50 lb) and bigger on the float.
- Real prices: about $1,350, plus $100 float line and $135 float.

- 18 pelagic fish a vessel at a time.

**Rubber net (§9)**
- Lands up to about 14 kg (30 lb).
- Hurts about 4% of the fish let go from it.
- $85 to $95.

**VHF (§10)**
- Two 8 ft (2.44 m), 6 dB whips, one at each after corner of the hardtop.
